Introduction

Plastics have become integral to modern life because they are lightweight, durable, versatile, and relatively inexpensive. They are used in packaging, construction, transport, healthcare, agriculture, textiles, and household goods. These same properties create an environmental challenge when products are discarded after short periods of use: many conventional plastics persist for long periods and can fragment into progressively smaller particles rather than readily disappearing. Plastic pollution therefore reflects not only the quantity of plastic produced and consumed, but also the design of products, patterns of use, collection systems, and the capacity to recover or safely manage discarded materials.

In India, the challenge is shaped by rapid urbanisation, large and diverse consumption patterns, extensive use of plastic packaging, and substantial differences in municipal capacity between and within states. The Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB) compiles plastic-waste estimates from information supplied by State Pollution Control Boards and Pollution Control Committees. A parliamentary reply reported 4,136,188.83 tonnes of plastic waste for 2022–2023, compared with 3,360,043.45 tonnes in 2018–2019. These figures are administrative estimates and should be interpreted in light of reporting coverage and methodology; they are not a direct measurement of all plastic entering the environment.¹,²

Plastic waste that is not collected or adequately contained can be transported through drains, streams, and rivers to lakes, estuaries, beaches, and the sea. Larger items may injure or entangle animals, while fragmentation produces microplastics, commonly defined as plastic particles smaller than 5 mm. Research in India has documented microplastics in inland waters and marine environments, although comparisons between studies are difficult because sampling locations, seasons, size limits, extraction methods, and analytical techniques differ.³,⁴,⁹–¹¹ This review summarises the principal environmental pathways and impacts, examines the Indian policy framework, and discusses practical responses that can reduce plastic leakage.

Sources and Pathways of Plastic Pollution

Packaging and short-lived consumer products: Packaging is a major part of the plastic-waste challenge because it is often used briefly and discarded immediately after a product is opened or consumed. Carry bags, wrappers, sachets, food containers, bottles, multilayer laminates, and delivery packaging can become litter when collection is absent or unreliable. The material composition of packaging matters: clean, single-polymer items may be easier to sort and recycle, whereas composite and multilayer materials can be technically and economically difficult to recover. Product redesign and reduction of unnecessary packaging can therefore prevent waste before it enters the collection system.

Mismanaged collection and transport: Plastic leakage occurs when waste is littered, dumped in open areas, left in overflowing bins, or carried from streets and markets into storm-water drains. Flooding and monsoon runoff can mobilise accumulated debris and move it into rivers and coastal waters. Open burning, although sometimes used where collection and disposal options are limited, is not an environmentally sound solution and can release hazardous combustion products. The Government of India has explicitly placed responsibility on urban local bodies and gram panchayats for plastic-waste management and for preventing open burning under the applicable rules.¹

Microplastic sources: Microplastics may be primary particles released in small form or secondary particles produced when larger plastic items break down through sunlight, mechanical abrasion, and weathering. Fibres released during washing of synthetic textiles, particles from the wear of tyres and other products, paint fragments, and industrial pellets are potential pathways. In aquatic environments, microplastics can be transported with water and sediment, deposited in lakes and estuaries, or ingested by organisms. A review of Indian inland aquatic ecosystems emphasised the importance of seasonal variation and hydrological conditions, including flow and discharge, when interpreting measured concentrations.³

Environmental Impacts

Terrestrial ecosystems and urban environments: Plastic litter can obstruct drains and contribute to local waterlogging, create hazards for livestock and wildlife, and reduce the quality of public spaces. In soils, plastic fragments can alter physical structure and interact with soil organisms; however, the magnitude and direction of effects depend on polymer type, particle size, concentration, soil conditions, and exposure duration. Plastic debris may also be transported from agricultural and peri-urban areas into waterways. These impacts reinforce the need to manage waste at the point of generation rather than relying only on periodic clean-up campaigns.

Freshwater, estuarine, and marine ecosystems: Rivers and drainage networks connect inland sources of plastic waste with downstream ecosystems. Studies reviewed by Singh and colleagues found evidence of microplastics in Indian inland waters, sediments, and selected aquatic organisms, while also identifying substantial gaps in standardised, repeated sampling.³ A 2024 review of Indian marine research reported that the evidence base is concentrated in certain coastal states and that research coverage remains uneven.⁴,¹⁰ These findings caution against treating a small number of site measurements as representative of the entire country.

Plastic debris can affect wildlife through ingestion, entanglement, and habitat disturbance. Ingestion may cause physical blockage, reduced feeding, or internal injury in some species. Microplastics may be ingested by plankton, invertebrates, and fish, creating opportunities for transfer through food webs. Laboratory and field studies report a range of biological responses, but the ecological significance of exposure depends on particle characteristics and real-world concentrations. The presence of plastic in an organism does not, by itself, demonstrate population-level harm; more long-term and ecologically realistic studies are needed.

Microplastics and human health: Human exposure to microplastics may occur through food, drinking water, and inhalation of airborne particles. The World Health Organization's assessment of microplastics in drinking water identified important uncertainties in occurrence data, measurement methods, and health-risk evidence, and called for improved research and monitoring.⁵ It is therefore important to distinguish evidence of exposure from proof of specific clinical outcomes. Current knowledge does not justify overstating the health effects of everyday exposure, but it supports precautionary measures that reduce avoidable plastic releases and improve water and environmental monitoring.

Plastic Waste Management in India

The principal national framework is the Plastic Waste Management Rules, 2016, issued under the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986, and subsequently amended. The rules assign responsibilities to waste generators, local authorities, producers, importers, brand owners, and plastic-waste processors. Amendments in 2021 prohibited specified single-use plastic items with low utility and high littering potential from 1 July 2022. The 2022 amendments introduced extended producer responsibility (EPR) guidelines for plastic packaging, including obligations relating to collection and processing.⁶–⁸

Government reporting provides evidence of both institutional activity and implementation challenges. A December 2024 parliamentary reply stated that 2,614 plastic-waste processors had been registered under EPR guidelines and that approximately 10.3 million tonnes of plastic packaging waste had been processed according to the centralised EPR portal. The same reply reported 4,446 material recovery facilities for solid waste, with a stated capacity of 31,427.2 tonnes per day.¹ These are important administrative indicators, but they should not be interpreted as proof that all plastic waste is collected, recycled into equivalent material, or prevented from leaking into the environment. Transparent verification, consistent reporting, and information on final processing routes are essential.

Implementation is difficult because local bodies differ in staffing, finance, land availability, collection coverage, and access to processing markets. Source segregation is especially important: mixed waste can contaminate recyclable plastics and increase sorting costs. Some plastic products have low resale value or are technically difficult to recycle, and long transport distances can make recovery uneconomic. Informal waste pickers and scrap dealers already perform collection and sorting functions in many locations. Policies that overlook these workers risk undermining livelihoods and losing practical recovery capacity. Inclusive systems should recognise, organise, and protect waste workers while improving occupational safety and access to formal services.

Sustainable Solutions and Recommendations

Reduce and redesign at source: The most reliable way to prevent plastic pollution is to avoid unnecessary material use. Public institutions, retailers, schools, and event organisers can reduce avoidable single-use items, promote refill and reuse systems, and choose products with durable or recyclable packaging where appropriate. Producers can redesign packaging to reduce material use, avoid unnecessary layers, improve labelling, and favour materials that can be collected and processed in existing systems. Substitution should be assessed carefully: an alternative is not automatically more sustainable unless its full life cycle, durability, local availability, and end-of-life pathway are considered.

Strengthen collection, segregation, and recycling: Households and institutions should separate dry recyclable waste from wet and residual waste. Local authorities need dependable door-to-door collection, accessible community collection points, material recovery facilities, and reliable onward transport to authorised processors. Recycling systems should prioritise safe, traceable mechanical recycling where material quality and product safety allow it. Recycling targets must be supported by verified processing capacity and end markets; otherwise, nominal recovery can mask stockpiling, disposal, or misreporting. Open burning and uncontrolled dumping should be prevented through enforcement and practical alternatives.

Make extended producer responsibility effective: EPR can shift part of the financial and organisational responsibility for packaging waste toward producers, importers, and brand owners. For it to drive genuine environmental improvement, registration and certificates should be supported by independent audits, traceability of waste flows, transparent public reporting, and penalties for false claims. EPR design should reward prevention, reuse, and high-quality recycling rather than simply the generation of certificates. The system should also support collection infrastructure and fair integration of informal-sector workers.

Monitor rivers, coasts, and microplastics: India would benefit from a coordinated monitoring framework using consistent sampling protocols, clear particle-size thresholds, quality assurance, and comparable analytical methods. Monitoring should cover water, sediment, biota, and relevant wastewater pathways, with repeated sampling across seasons. Researchers should report polymer identification methods and contamination controls, and should distinguish particle counts from mass-based measurements. Hydrological data such as rainfall, flow velocity, and discharge should be recorded so that concentrations can be interpreted in terms of transport and loads.³

Education and community participation: Schools and colleges can connect environmental education with practical action: waste audits, segregation demonstrations, reusable alternatives, neighbourhood clean-ups, and local mapping of litter hotspots. Clean-up activities should be paired with source prevention and safe handling rather than treated as a substitute for municipal services. Public communication should explain which materials are accepted locally, how to prepare them for collection, and why littering and burning are harmful. Community participation is most effective when local authorities provide reliable services and feedback on outcomes.

Discussion

The evidence reviewed indicates that plastic pollution in India is a systems problem involving product design, consumption, waste collection, processing markets, governance, and environmental transport. National data demonstrate a substantial reported waste stream, while studies of inland and marine environments confirm that microplastics are present in multiple aquatic settings. At the same time, differences in monitoring methods and geographic coverage limit direct comparisons and national extrapolation. Policy instruments such as single-use plastic restrictions and EPR provide a foundation, but their effectiveness depends on implementation quality, credible verification, and the availability of practical alternatives.

A balanced approach should follow the waste hierarchy: prevent and reduce first, reuse where feasible, recycle suitable materials, and ensure safe treatment or disposal of residual waste. This is consistent with the United Nations Environment Programme's systems-change approach, which emphasises reducing problematic plastics, expanding reuse, improving recycling, and diversifying materials and delivery systems.⁹ Recycling remains necessary, but it cannot compensate indefinitely for growth in avoidable single-use consumption or weak collection systems.

This review has limitations. It is a narrative synthesis rather than a registered systematic review or meta-analysis. It does not calculate a pooled national microplastic concentration, independently audit government administrative data, or assess every state-level policy and local programme. The available Indian literature is geographically and methodologically uneven, and human-health evidence remains uncertain. These limitations mean that recommendations should be treated as priorities for policy and further study, not as quantified estimates of intervention effectiveness.

Conclusions

Plastic pollution in India is driven by high-volume use of short-lived products, gaps in collection and segregation, limited recovery options for some materials, and the movement of discarded plastics through terrestrial and aquatic environments. Government rules and EPR mechanisms provide an important regulatory foundation, but outcomes depend on effective local implementation, transparent monitoring, and inclusive waste-management systems. Reducing unnecessary plastic, expanding reuse, improving collection and sorting, verifying recycling, protecting waste workers, and standardising environmental monitoring offer a practical path toward lower plastic leakage and more sustainable resource use.
